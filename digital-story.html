More than
clay.

The story of one pottery studio, and what can grow when people create together.

Rebecca Graham hadn't planned to find the future home of her pottery studio while walking near Weaver Street Market. Drawn to Carrboro's lively downtown and creative atmosphere, she had been exploring the town when she spotted a building for lease. She reached out to the property manager, setting in motion what would become Clayboro Studios, which opened in April 2026. For Graham, the goal extended beyond offering pottery classes. She wanted to build a space where people could create, learn and connect with their community.

Her experience raises a larger question: What can creative spaces mean for the people who use them?

THE PERSONAL STORY

Before Clayboro

Rebecca's connection to pottery began with a ceramics class in high school. She fell in love with the craft almost immediately and continued returning to it over the next 13 years, even as school and travel took her in other directions. Although she once hoped to study pottery in college, she ultimately pursued international studies instead. Still, ceramics remained a constant in her life.

Eventually, Graham found her way to a pottery studio in St. Petersburg, Florida, where she started as an unpaid intern and worked her way up to studio manager. There, she saw how a shared creative space could bring together “people from all walks of life.” Members came to work with clay, but the studio also offered opportunities to meet others, exchange ideas and build relationships. Seeing that community develop made the work especially meaningful to Graham and helped inspire her decision to open a studio of her own.

INSIDE THE STUDIO

Once Graham decided to pursue her own studio, things moved quickly. She signed the lease for the Carrboro building the day after moving to North Carolina, and just a month later, Clayboro Studios was open. What had started with an unexpected discovery near Weaver Street Market had become a business of her own, bringing together years of experience and a new beginning in an unfamiliar town.

That transition is at the center of A Place to Take Shape, a short documentary exploring Graham’s journey to opening Clayboro. Having experienced the community that formed around the studio she managed in Florida, she now had the opportunity to create something similar in Carrboro. Through her story, the film explores not only the challenges of starting a business, but also the role a shared creative space can play in bringing people together.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

The Social Side of Creativity.

The sense of community Graham experienced at her previous studio reflects a broader connection between creative activities and social interaction. While people may initially turn to the arts to learn a skill or express themselves, these activities can also create opportunities to meet others, build relationships and become more involved in their communities.

Research from the National Endowment for the Arts suggests that arts participation is associated with stronger social connections. In a 2024 report examining national survey data, researchers found that 29.2% of adults who recently attended arts events participated in club or organization meetings at least 12 times in the previous year, compared with 15% of non-attendees. Arts attendees were also more likely to report spending time with friends and family and rarely feeling lonely.

Although these findings do not prove that participating in the arts directly improves social well-being, they highlight the potential importance of shared creative experiences. For spaces like Clayboro, that connection raises another question: Could the act of making pottery together offer similar benefits?

A CLOSER LOOK

What Happens When We Create Together?

While national research suggests a connection between arts participation and social well-being, a smaller study offers a closer look at the role of clay-making specifically. In 2021, researchers examined whether group art therapy could help reduce loneliness and hopelessness among 60 older adults living alone. Over six weeks, half of the participants attended guided clay-making sessions, while the other half served as a comparison group.

By the end of the study, participants in the clay-making group reported lower average loneliness and hopelessness scores than those in the comparison group. Although the study focused on therapeutic sessions rather than a community pottery studio, its findings offer another perspective on the potential benefits of creating alongside others. For Graham, who witnessed the connections that developed at her previous studio, the research adds context to the kind of environment she hopes to foster at Clayboro.

WHY THIS STORY MATTERS

More Than the Finished Piece.

When Graham opened Clayboro, she knew the kind of studio she wanted to create, but she couldn't predict the relationships that might develop within it. What began as a spontaneous decision to pursue a new opportunity in Carrboro became a chance to build the kind of creative community she had experienced in Florida.

The research on arts participation and clay-making suggests that creative activities can offer benefits beyond learning a skill or producing something tangible. For Graham, that possibility is part of what makes the work meaningful. While the finished pottery may be what members take home, the conversations, shared experiences and connections formed along the way can be just as valuable.

Clayboro's story is still unfolding, but Graham's experience highlights something larger about creative spaces: Their value isn't always measured by what people make, but also by what they find in the process.
